\begin{abstract}
\textsc{multiview} is a small, dependency-free C99 library for metric scene
analysis and 3D model extraction from the synchronized video streams of
\emph{two stationary cameras} observing an overlapping volume, optionally
augmented by a collection of still photographs and, in a later stage,
range-finder measurements. Calibration is part of the system: the rig is
calibrated from views of a printed letter-page checkerboard of known square
size, or from a computer display under our control showing synchronized
Gray-code patterns. This document doubles as a self-study overview of the
underlying mathematics: each concept is developed from first principles,
cross-linked to the modules that implement it, illustrated by a
\emph{hand-computable} worked example, and sourced in the bibliography.
All quantitative claims (\autoref{sec:results}) are reproducible from the
deterministic test programs in the repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and capabilities}
\label{sec:capabilities}

The target deployment is deliberately narrow and fully under our control: two
cameras are rigidly mounted and observe the same working volume. The system
first \emph{calibrates itself} from views of a known planar target
(\autoref{sec:calib}); thereafter video is processed frame by frame. Because
the cameras do not move, every geometric quantity that depends only on the
rig --- projection matrices, the fundamental matrix, the rectifying
homographies --- is computed \emph{once} and reused for every frame, which is
the central efficiency win of the stationary-rig assumption.

The implemented core (v0.2.0) provides:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item plane-based calibration of intrinsics, distortion, and pose from a
        printed letter-page checkerboard or from a controlled display
        showing Gray-code patterns (\autoref{sec:calib});
  \item a calibrated pinhole camera model with Brown radial--tangential lens
        distortion, forward projection and ray back-projection
        (\autoref{sec:camera});
  \item exact two-view epipolar geometry from the calibration, and the
        normalized 8-point algorithm to estimate or verify it from image
        correspondences (\autoref{sec:epipolar});
  \item $N$-view linear triangulation of corresponding image points into
        metric 3D, with reprojection-error reporting
        (\autoref{sec:triangulation});
  \item planar stereo rectification of the pair, and dense correspondence by
        block matching with sub-pixel refinement, giving per-pixel depth
        (\autoref{sec:stereo});
  \item point-cloud assembly and PLY export for inspection in standard mesh
        tools, plus minimal PGM image I/O (\autoref{sec:models}).
\end{enumerate}

Planned extensions --- structure-from-motion over an auxiliary photo
collection and fusion of range-finder data --- are designed but not yet
implemented; their theory and integration points are described in
\autoref{sec:extensions} so that the code can grow into them without
rearchitecting.

\paragraph{How to read this document.} \autoref{sec:toolkit} collects the
small amount of linear algebra everything else stands on.
\autoref{sec:camera}--\autoref{sec:stereo} then follow the data through the
system: camera model, calibration, two-view geometry, triangulation, dense
stereo. Every section ends in a worked example with numbers chosen so the
computation fits on paper; doing them by hand \emph{is} the intended
self-study exercise, and each can then be checked against the library.

\paragraph{Design constraints.} The library is strict C99 with no
dependencies beyond \code{libm}, row-major \code{double} arrays throughout,
and fully deterministic (no clocks, no ambient randomness; the demos use
fixed-seed generators). This keeps it portable from desktop to embedded
targets, and keeps every experiment repeatable --- a prerequisite for the
controlled-environment, learning-oriented use the project is intended for.

Processing has a one-time phase and a per-frame phase:
\[
\underbrace{\text{target views}\rightarrow\text{calibrate}}_{\text{once, \code{target}/\code{graycode}/\code{calib}}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\text{2 frames}\rightarrow\text{undistort}\rightarrow\text{rectify}\rightarrow\text{match}\rightarrow\text{depth}\rightarrow\text{cloud}}_{\text{per frame, \code{cam}/\code{rectify}/\code{stereo}/\code{triangulate}/\code{cloud}}}
\]
Sparse operation (feature correspondences instead of dense matching) uses the
same chain with \code{epipolar} providing the correspondence-validation gate.
Because the rig is static, temporal accumulation over video frames is a
simple union of per-frame clouds in the fixed world frame; moving objects
appear as temporally inconsistent points and can be segmented by comparing
frames --- the entry point for scene \emph{analysis} beyond reconstruction.

\section{Mathematical toolkit}
\label{sec:toolkit}

Four ideas carry the whole system; everything later is an application.

\subsection{Homogeneous coordinates}
A 2D point $(u,v)$ is represented as any triple $(wu, wv, w)$, $w\neq0$; a
3D point $(X,Y,Z)$ as $(X,Y,Z,1)$ up to scale. The payoff: projection,
homographies and rigid motions all become \emph{linear} maps, and points at
infinity ($w=0$) need no special case. We write $\simeq$ for equality up to
a nonzero scale.

\begin{example}[dehomogenization]
\label{ex:homog}
$(120,\,140,\,2) \simeq (60,\,70,\,1)$: both name the pixel $(60,70)$.
Conversely the direction ``straight down the $u$ axis'' is $(1,0,0)$, which
no finite pixel represents.
\end{example}

\subsection{Rotations}
A rotation matrix $\R\in SO(3)$ satisfies $\R^{\!\top}\R=\mat{I}$,
$\det\R=1$; its rows are the axes of the new frame expressed in the old.
Consequently $\R^{-1}=\R^{\!\top}$ --- inverting a rigid transform never
requires a matrix inversion.

\begin{example}[rotation about $y$]
\label{ex:rot}
$\R_y(90^\circ)=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&-1\\0&1&0\\1&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$
sends the world $z$ axis $(0,0,1)$ to $(-1,0,0)$: a camera yawed $90^\circ$
sees the world's $-x$ direction ahead. Check $\R^{\!\top}\R=\mat I$ by
inspection: the rows are orthonormal.
\end{example}

\subsection{The cross-product matrix}
For $\vect v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)$,
\begin{equation}
[\vect v]_\times=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-v_3&v_2\\ v_3&0&-v_1\\ -v_2&v_1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad [\vect v]_\times\vect w=\vect v\times\vect w .
\label{eq:skew}
\end{equation}
It is rank 2 with null vector $\vect v$ itself --- the algebraic reason
epipoles exist (\autoref{sec:epipolar}).

\begin{example}[skew matrix]
\label{ex:skew}
$\vect v=(1,0,0)$:
$[\vect v]_\times=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$,
and $[\vect v]_\times(0,1,0)^{\!\top}=(0,0,1)^{\!\top}=\vect v\times\vect w$. \qedhere
\end{example}

\subsection{Homogeneous least squares and the SVD}
Every estimation problem in this system reduces to: given $\mat A$
($m\times n$), find $\vect x\neq0$ minimizing $\|\mat A\vect x\|$ subject to
$\|\vect x\|=1$. The minimizer is the right singular vector of the smallest
singular value of $\mat A=\mat U\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1\ge\dots\ge\sigma_n)\,\mat V^{\!\top}$
\cite{GolubVanLoan2013}. The library computes this with a one-sided Jacobi
SVD (\autoref{sec:numerics}) via \code{mv\_nullvec}.

\begin{example}[null vector by hand]
\label{ex:nullvec}
$\mat A=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ has rank 1
(row 2 $=2\times$ row 1), so the smallest singular value is exactly $0$ and
the null vector solves $x_1+2x_2=0$: $\vect x=\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(2,-1)$.
Check: $\mat A\vect x=\vect 0$.
\end{example}

\section{Camera model}
\label{sec:camera}

A camera is a pinhole with intrinsic matrix
\begin{equation}
\K = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & s & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:K}
\end{equation}
rotation $\R$ and translation $\tv$ mapping world points into the camera
frame, $\X_c = \R\X + \tv$; the camera center is $\Cv = -\R^{\!\top}\tv$. A
world point projects to the homogeneous pixel
\begin{equation}
\x \;\simeq\; \PP \begin{pmatrix}\X\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\PP = \K\,[\,\R \mid \tv\,]
\label{eq:pinhole}
\end{equation}
\cite{HartleyZisserman2004}. Real lenses deviate from \eqref{eq:pinhole}; on
normalized coordinates $(x,y)=(X_c/Z_c,\,Y_c/Z_c)$ with $r^2=x^2{+}y^2$ the
Brown model gives the distorted
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x_d &= x\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + 2p_1 xy + p_2(r^2 + 2x^2),\\
y_d &= y\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + p_1(r^2 + 2y^2) + 2p_2 xy,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:distortion}
\end{equation}
and the pixel is $\K(x_d,y_d,1)^{\!\top}$. Back-projection inverts
\eqref{eq:distortion} by fixed-point iteration and returns the ray
$\Cv + s\,\vect d$, $s>0$.

\begin{example}[projection]
\label{ex:project}
Let $f_x{=}f_y{=}100$, $c_x{=}c_y{=}50$, $s{=}0$, $\R=\mat I$, $\tv=\vect0$,
no distortion, and $\X=(1,2,10)$. Normalized coordinates:
$(x,y)=(1/10,\,2/10)=(0.1,0.2)$. Pixel:
$u=100\cdot0.1+50=60$, $v=100\cdot0.2+50=70$ --- the point lands at
$(60,70)$, cf.~\autoref{ex:homog}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[camera center]
\label{ex:center}
Let $\R=\R_y(90^\circ)$ from \autoref{ex:rot} and $\tv=(3,0,0)$. Then
$\R^{\!\top}\tv=(0,0,-3)$ and $\Cv=-\R^{\!\top}\tv=(0,0,3)$: the camera sits
three units down the world $z$ axis. Check forward:
$\R\Cv+\tv=(0\!\cdot\!0+0\!\cdot\!0-1\!\cdot\!3,\,0,\,0\!\cdot\!3)+(3,0,0)
=(-3,0,0)+(3,0,0)=\vect0$ --- the center maps to the camera origin, as it
must.
\end{example}

\subsection{The temporal dimension: a camera is also a clock}
\label{sec:timemodel}

Equations \eqref{eq:pinhole}--\eqref{eq:distortion} describe \emph{where}
a camera maps a world point; a complete model must also say \emph{when}.
Each camera runs on its own oscillator, so frame $k$ (and, for a
rolling-shutter sensor, row $r$ within it) is captured at the world time
\begin{equation}
t_i(k, r) \;=\; \phi_i \;+\; k\,T_i \;+\; r\,\tau_i \;+\; \tfrac12 t_e,
\label{eq:clock}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_i$ is the camera's clock offset, $T_i$ its true frame period
(consumer crystals deviate from nominal by 50--100\,ppm, so $T_i$ differs
per camera and $\phi_i$ drifts), $\tau_i$ the row-readout time (zero for
global shutter), and $t_e$ the exposure whose midpoint is the effective
sampling instant. The full camera state is therefore
$(\K,\R,\tv,\,k_{1..5};\;\phi,T,\tau,t_e)$ --- geometry \emph{and}
clock --- and a measurement is not a pixel but a \emph{timestamped}
pixel: a ray with a time attached.

The multi-view machinery of
\autoref{sec:epipolar}--\autoref{sec:stereo} silently assumes
simultaneity: the epipolar constraint \eqref{eq:epiconstraint} and the
triangulation stack \eqref{eq:dlt} relate observations \emph{of the same
world state}. For static scenes the assumption is vacuous; for a point
moving at velocity $\vect v$, a timestamp mismatch $\Delta t$ between the
paired observations displaces one of them by $\vect v\,\Delta t$ ---
producing epipolar residual and phantom depth in exactly the way
quantified in \autoref{ex:sync}. Simultaneity, like zero distortion, is
thus an idealization to be \emph{calibrated}: the clock parameters of
\eqref{eq:clock} are estimable state, not assumptions --- the display
fixture measures them directly through a dedicated temporal calibration
signal (\autoref{sec:tempcalib}) and moving scene content re-estimates
them at run time, with the measurement and correction practice developed
in \autoref{sec:softsync}. When all cameras share a hardware trigger,
$\phi$, $T$ collapse to common values and the temporal model disappears
back into the geometric one --- the trigger is, in this language, a way
of buying \eqref{eq:clock} exactly rather than estimating it.

\section{Calibration from planar targets}
\label{sec:calib}

Calibration is part of the system, not an external input. Both supported
targets are \emph{planes with known metric structure}: a printed
checkerboard page (\autoref{sec:printed}) and a controlled computer display
(\autoref{sec:display}). The mathematics is the same for both: several views
of a known plane determine the intrinsics, the distortion, and each view's
pose, by Zhang's method \cite{Zhang2000,SturmMaybank1999}.

\subsection{The homography of a plane}
\label{sec:homography}

Put the target plane at $Z=0$ in its own frame. Then \eqref{eq:pinhole}
collapses: writing $\R=[\vect r_1\,\vect r_2\,\vect r_3]$ (columns),
\begin{equation}
\x \simeq \K\,[\vect r_1\;\vect r_2\;\tv]\,(X,Y,1)^{\!\top}
\;=\; \Hm\,(X,Y,1)^{\!\top},
\label{eq:planehom}
\end{equation}
a $3\times3$ \emph{homography} with 8 degrees of freedom. Each point
correspondence gives two linear equations in the entries of $\Hm$, so
$n\ge4$ points determine it as a homogeneous least-squares problem
(\autoref{ex:nullvec} at scale $2n\times9$), solved by normalized DLT
(\code{mv\_homography\_dlt}; normalization as in \autoref{sec:numerics}).

\begin{example}[plane homography]
\label{ex:homography}
Frontal camera: $\K$ as in \autoref{ex:project}, $\R=\mat I$,
$\tv=(0,0,2)$. Then $[\vect r_1\,\vect r_2\,\tv]=
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and
\[
\Hm=\K\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}100&0&100\\0&100&100\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}
\simeq\begin{pmatrix}50&0&50\\0&50&50\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The plane point $(X,Y)=(0.1,0.2)$ maps to
$(50\cdot0.1+50,\;50\cdot0.2+50)=(55,60)$. Check via
\autoref{ex:project}: the 3D point is $(0.1,0.2,2)$, normalized
$(0.05,0.1)$, pixel $(100\cdot0.05+50,\,100\cdot0.1+50)=(55,60)$. \qedhere
\end{example}

\subsection{Zhang's constraints and the closed-form intrinsics}
\label{sec:zhang}

Write $\Hm=[\vect h_1\,\vect h_2\,\vect h_3]$ (columns). From
\eqref{eq:planehom}, $\vect h_1\simeq\K\vect r_1$ and
$\vect h_2\simeq\K\vect r_2$ with the \emph{same} scale. Since
$\vect r_1,\vect r_2$ are orthonormal,
\begin{equation}
\vect h_1^{\!\top}\B\,\vect h_2=0,
\qquad
\vect h_1^{\!\top}\B\,\vect h_1=\vect h_2^{\!\top}\B\,\vect h_2,
\qquad
\B \;\coloneqq\; \K^{-\!\top}\K^{-1},
\label{eq:zhangconstraints}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ each view of the plane contributes two linear equations in the six
independent entries of the symmetric matrix $\B$ (the \emph{image of the
absolute conic}). Three views generically determine $\B$ up to scale; two
suffice if zero skew ($s{=}0 \Leftrightarrow B_{12}{=}0$) is imposed. The
solution is again a null vector (\autoref{ex:nullvec}); $\K$ then follows
from $\B$ in closed form \cite{Zhang2000}, and the pose of each view is
recovered as
\begin{equation}
\lambda=\frac1{\|\K^{-1}\vect h_1\|},\quad
\vect r_1=\lambda\K^{-1}\vect h_1,\quad
\vect r_2=\lambda\K^{-1}\vect h_2,\quad
\vect r_3=\vect r_1\times\vect r_2,\quad
\tv=\lambda\K^{-1}\vect h_3,
\label{eq:extrinsics}
\end{equation}
with $[\vect r_1\,\vect r_2\,\vect r_3]$ snapped to the nearest true
rotation via SVD (noise makes it only approximately orthonormal).

\begin{example}[pose from a homography, by hand]
\label{ex:zhang}
Take the (unscaled) $\Hm$ of \autoref{ex:homography} and the known
$\K$. Then
\[
\K^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0.01&0&-0.5\\0&0.01&-0.5\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
\K^{-1}\vect h_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\K^{-1}\vect h_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\K^{-1}\vect h_3=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both constraint equations of \eqref{eq:zhangconstraints} hold:
$\vect r_1\cdot\vect r_2 = 0$ and $\|\vect r_1\|=\|\vect r_2\|$. The scale
is $\lambda=1/\|(1,0,0)\|=1$, so \eqref{eq:extrinsics} gives
$\vect r_1=(1,0,0)$, $\vect r_2=(0,1,0)$,
$\vect r_3=\vect r_1\times\vect r_2=(0,0,1)$, $\tv=(0,0,2)$ --- exactly the
frontal pose two metres from the target that we started from.
\end{example}

\subsection{Radial distortion and alternation}
\label{sec:radial}

With $\K$ and poses in hand, the ideal (undistorted) projection $(u,v)$ of
every target point is known, and \eqref{eq:distortion} predicts the observed
$(u_d,v_d)$ linearly in $(k_1,k_2)$:
\begin{equation}
u_d-u=(u-c_x)(k_1r^2+k_2r^4),\qquad
v_d-v=(v-c_y)(k_1r^2+k_2r^4),
\label{eq:radiallin}
\end{equation}
a least-squares problem with two unknowns. Since the homographies were fit
to \emph{distorted} points, the library alternates: fit geometry, estimate
$(k_1,k_2)$, undistort the observations, refit --- a few rounds converge for
moderate distortion \cite{Zhang2000}.

An identifiability caveat worth internalizing: over the small radius range a
letter-page target covers, $r^2$ and $r^4$ are nearly proportional, so
$k_1$ and $k_2$ are individually ill-determined even when their combined
\emph{curve} $k_1r^2{+}k_2r^4$ --- the thing that actually corrects pixels
--- is fit to well below noise (measured in \autoref{sec:results}). Wide
targets, or the full-field display target of \autoref{sec:display}, are the
cure when the individual coefficients matter.

\subsection{The printed target}
\label{sec:printed}

The standard passive target is a checkerboard printed on a letter/A4 page:
$8\times10$ squares of nominally 24\,mm ($192\times240$\,mm), i.e.\
$7\times9$ inner corners (\code{mv/target.h};
\code{mv\_target\_render\_pgm} emits the printable image --- at 10\,px/mm a
24\,mm square is 240\,px). Printers rescale, so the procedure is
\emph{print, then measure one square} and pass the measured size; only that
measurement makes the calibration metric. Corner \emph{positions} in the
image are currently supplied by the caller (an external detector or manual
marking); a saddle-point corner detector is on the roadmap
(\autoref{sec:roadmap}). Ten to twenty views with strong tilt diversity
($\pm30$--$40^\circ$) condition the focal length well --- near-frontal views
leave it weakly observable, a property visible in the experiment of
\autoref{sec:results}.

\subsection{The display as an active target}
\label{sec:display}

A computer display we control is a better target than paper wherever one is
available: its pixel grid is a plane of exactly known geometry (pixel pitch
from the panel datasheet, or measured once), and because we control what it
shows \emph{when}, correspondence needs no detector at all. The display
plays, synchronized with capture, a sequence of binary stripe patterns that
encode each display pixel's coordinate in \emph{Gray code}
\cite{Inokuchi1984,Geng2011}: bit $b$ of the code is shown as stripes,
followed by its inverse, for $b=0..\lceil\log_2 W\rceil{-}1$, then the same
for the $y$ axis. A camera pixel observing the display reads its bit as
``brighter in the pattern or in the inverse?'' --- a per-pixel comparison
immune to illumination, display brightness and gamma --- and after all
frames has decoded \emph{which display pixel it sees}
(\code{mv/graycode.h}). This yields thousands of dense, uniformly spread
correspondences per view; the same pipeline
(\autoref{sec:homography}--\autoref{sec:radial}) consumes them, with plane
coordinates $=$ display pixel $\times$ pitch. Gray code rather than plain
binary because adjacent stripes differ in exactly one bit: a threshold error
at a stripe boundary displaces the decoded coordinate by at most one pixel
instead of, e.g., $2^{b}$.

This static sweep is operating mode M1 of the calibration subsystem
(\autoref{sec:calibspec}).

\begin{example}[Gray code by hand]
\label{ex:gray}
Encode $v=6$: binary $110$; Gray $=110\oplus011=101$. Decode $g=101$ by
prefix-XOR: $b_2=1$, $b_1=1\oplus0=1$, $b_0=1\oplus0\oplus1=0$ ---
binary $110=6$. Now flip the last Gray bit (a boundary
misread): $g'=100\rightarrow$ binary $111=7$, an error of exactly one
position. In plain binary, flipping the top bit of $110$ gives $010=2$, an
error of four.
\end{example}

\subsection{Design: the rotating display}
\label{sec:rotating}

For an $N$-camera rig (\autoref{sec:scaling}) the planned calibration
fixture is the display of \autoref{sec:display} mounted on a plate rotating
at constant angular velocity $\omega$, so that the screen sweeps past every
camera each revolution. This subsection records the design; implementation
is a roadmap item (\autoref{sec:roadmap}).

\paragraph{What the rotation provides.} Three things, all substantial.
(i) \emph{Tilt diversity per camera}: as the screen sweeps past, every
frame shows the known plane at a different orientation --- exactly the
varied-tilt view set Zhang's method needs, whose absence measurably
degrades focal recovery (\autoref{sec:results}). (ii) \emph{One shared
orbit}: every camera observes the same one-parameter family of poses ---
screen pose $=$ mount offset composed with rotation about a fixed axis at
constant $\omega$. Fitting that orbit (axis, $\omega$, phase, mount;
$\sim$10 parameters) jointly across cameras links \emph{all} of them into
one world frame even if no two ever see the screen simultaneously: the
orbit itself is the common reference object, replacing pairwise shared
views. (iii) \emph{The display is the clock}: the screen broadcasts its
frame index, so every captured image self-reports display time and
cameras synchronize to the rotation model without a hardware trigger.

\paragraph{What the rotation forbids.} The static Gray-code stack of
\autoref{sec:display} requires one camera pixel to observe one display
point across $\sim$20 frames --- impossible on a moving target. The
pattern must therefore be \emph{single-shot self-identifying}: every frame
decodable alone. The design is layered:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a checkerboard-type \emph{saddle-point grid} as the metric layer
        --- saddle locations are unbiased under perspective and under
        (moderate, symmetric) blur, unlike dot centroids, which both
        foreshortening and blur displace;
  \item \emph{identity coding} embedded in the grid (marker bits in
        alternate squares, or an M-array whose every $k\times k$ window is
        unique), so partial and oblique views self-locate --- most frames
        of a sweep are partial;
  \item a \emph{frame-counter strip} along the border, Gray-coded
        \emph{in time} (successive indices differ in one bit,
        cf.~\autoref{ex:gray}), providing the timestamp for the orbit
        model;
  \item a \emph{temporal-calibration layer}: the counter timestamps are
        quantized to the display refresh, so a few dedicated
        \emph{phase patches} toggle black/white every display frame ---
        a camera whose exposure straddles a toggle records an
        intermediate gray level whose value is the sub-refresh phase of
        its shutter (and, as a by-product, its true exposure duration).
        Counter $+$ phase samples give every camera a stream of
        display-clock timestamps, from which each camera's clock
        \emph{offset and rate} are fit --- the display is not only the
        geometric reference but the rig's common clock
        (\autoref{sec:softsync});
  \item \emph{multi-scale structure} (a coarse grid nested with a fine
        one), since one display serves cameras at different distances and
        obliquities; strokes must span ${\ge}3$--$4$ pixels in the worst
        camera at the most oblique usable pose;
  \item \emph{binary black/white only}: LCD gamma and contrast shift
        off-axis, so gray-level codes degrade with viewing angle while
        binary features with per-frame identity do not.
\end{itemize}
Across frames, only the identity layer changes (counter strip; optionally
alternating coarse/fine emphasis) --- pose variation is already supplied by
the rotation. At low $\omega$ the screen moves ${<}1$ pixel between
consecutive frames, so pattern-plus-inverse in consecutive frames remains
usable and restores the per-pixel contrast robustness of
\autoref{sec:display}. The concrete arrangement of all layers on screen
is specified once, in \autoref{fig:layout} (\autoref{sec:calibspec}).

\begin{example}[motion-blur budget]
\label{ex:blur}
Image-space blur during exposure $t_e$ is
$\omega\,r\,f\,t_e/Z$ pixels, with $r$ the lever arm from axis to screen
edge. For the experimental rig's numbers ($f=800$\,px, $Z=4.7$\,m) with
$r=0.25$\,m and $\omega=6$\,rpm $=0.63$\,rad/s: the screen edge moves
$0.63\cdot0.25\cdot800/4.7\approx27$\,px/s, so a $5$\,ms exposure blurs
$0.13$\,px --- comfortably sub-pixel, and between frames at 30\,fps the
screen shifts $0.9$\,px, validating the consecutive-inverse-frame option.
The formula is the design knob linking $\omega$, exposure, and screen
brightness.
\end{example}

\paragraph{One mechanical trap: cant the screen.} A screen standing
parallel to the rotation axis presents orientations that all differ by
rotation about a \emph{single} axis --- a weak/singular family for
plane-based calibration \cite{SturmMaybank1999}, the same disease as the
measured near-frontal weakness. Mounting the screen tilted back
$20$--$30^\circ$ makes its normal sweep a cone instead of a circle,
exercising two rotation axes; this one bracket angle is the most important
mechanical detail of the fixture.

\paragraph{Pipeline.} Gate frames by obliquity (roughly $15$--$55^\circ$
off-normal: frontal frames are uninformative, grazing frames unmatchable);
per-frame homographies from the self-identifying grid; per-camera Zhang
intrinsics from the gated set; joint orbit fit for all extrinsics $+$
axis $+$ $\omega$ $+$ phase --- the natural first customer for the planned
Levenberg--Marquardt stage.

\subsection{Temporal calibration: the display as a clock signal}
\label{sec:tempcalib}

Geometric calibration shows the cameras a known \emph{spatial} structure;
the temporal camera model \eqref{eq:clock} is calibrated the same way,
by showing a known \emph{temporal} structure --- and the requirements
mirror \autoref{sec:display} exactly: the signal must be
\emph{unambiguous} (coded, not periodic-only, or time aliases the way an
uncoded stripe pattern aliases in space), \emph{fine} (containing
transitions faster than the precision sought), and \emph{covering}
(transitions must actually land inside camera exposures often enough to
be sampled). Each clock parameter of \eqref{eq:clock} gets its own layer
of the signal:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Offset $\phi$, coarse:} the Gray-coded frame counter
        (\autoref{sec:rotating}) --- an absolute, self-delimiting time
        word, readable in any single frame, quantized to the display
        refresh $T_d$.
  \item \emph{Offset $\phi$, fine:} phase patches toggling every display
        frame. A camera exposure of length $t_e$ that straddles a toggle
        integrates part old, part new value: the recorded gray level
        $g\in(0,1)$ \emph{is} the toggle's position inside the exposure,
        refining the timestamp below $T_d$. A display cannot stagger
        toggles \emph{within} a refresh --- every change lands on the
        vsync --- but the panel's own \emph{scanout} staggers them for
        free: rows are updated top-to-bottom across the refresh, so a
        patch at row $y$ toggles at
        $\text{vsync}+ (y/H)\,T_d$. Patches at several vertical
        positions therefore sample several sub-refresh phases (panel
        response time smears the edge; it is itself calibratable from
        the $g$ statistics). Each patch straddles with probability
        $t_e/T_d$ per frame; full coverage is not needed --- the
        camera--display beat sweeps the phase, and a one-minute fit
        collects hundreds of straddle samples. One additional patch
        toggling at half rate disambiguates exposures longer than
        $T_d$.
  \item \emph{Period $T$:} no extra signal needed --- a linear fit of
        decoded display time against camera frame index over seconds
        (\autoref{ex:skew}); the inevitable beat between camera and
        display rates is a \emph{feature}, sweeping the sampling phase
        through the display cycle so all phases get observed without any
        genlock.
  \item \emph{Row time $\tau$ (rolling shutter):} a full-screen toggle
        turns the rolling readout into its own measuring instrument ---
        rows exposed before the toggle record one value, rows after it
        the other, so the captured frame shows horizontal \emph{bands}
        whose pitch is the number of rows read per display period:
        $\tau = T_d / (\text{rows per band cycle})$, plus the readout
        direction for free.
  \item \emph{Exposure $t_e$:} recovered jointly with the fine phase ---
        the fraction of frames whose patches straddle, and the
        distribution of recorded gray levels, both depend on $t_e$ alone
        once $\phi, T$ are known.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[row time from banding]
\label{ex:banding}
A rolling-shutter camera photographs the display running a full-screen
toggle at $T_d=16.7$\,ms (bright/dark half-periods of $8.33$\,ms). The
captured frame shows bands $289$ rows tall. Then
$\tau = 8.33\,\text{ms}/289 \approx 28.8\,\mu$s per row --- and the full
$1080$-row readout takes $1080\cdot28.8\,\mu\text{s}\approx31$\,ms,
nearly the whole $33$\,ms frame period, which is typical: rolling
sensors read continuously. One captured frame of a blinking screen
measures the parameter that \autoref{ex:rolling} showed corrupting the
moving-target calibration --- and once $\tau$ is known, every row's
timestamp is known, turning the rolling-shutter \emph{bias} into just
more timestamped measurements per \autoref{sec:timemodel}.
\end{example}

The same session that calibrates geometry therefore calibrates every
clock parameter: one revolution of the fixture, read by all cameras,
yields $(\K,\R,\tv,k)$ from the corner grid and
$(\phi,T,\tau,t_e)$ from the temporal layers --- the display is the
rig's ruler and its metronome. Run-time maintenance of the clock model
without the display (movers, LED beacon, golden pairs) is the practice
material of \autoref{sec:softsync}; the concrete screen layout, operating
modes, and data contract are concentrated in \autoref{sec:calibspec}.

\paragraph{M-array construction.} One bit per grid square
($19\times10=190$ bits): a black center dot on white squares, a white
dot on black squares, so every square carries a bit and no dot
approaches a saddle corner ($\ge28$\,px clearance). The window size
must be $4\times4$: with $3\times3$ windows the $136$ windows
$\times\,4$ rotations demand $544$ distinct 9-bit codes and only $512$
exist --- pigeonhole forbids it. At $4\times4$, the $112$ windows
$\times\,4$ rotations need $448$ of $2^{16}$ codes, ample slack. The
array is found once by seeded search with greedy repair (seed 20260803,
solved in 30 steps) and shipped as a constant table in \code{libmv}
(\code{src/pattern.c}), with the exhaustively verified properties: all
$448$ rotated window codes are pairwise distinct with Hamming distance
$\ge2$. The rotation clause is what resolves the checkerboard's 4-fold
orientation ambiguity: any single decoded window fixes the global
orientation, after which corner ids are assigned in the pattern frame.

\paragraph{Reader algorithms.} Fixed choices, so two implementations
decode identically:
\emph{binarization} --- local mean over a $31\times31$ box (integral
image) with $\pm$hysteresis; black/white radiometric references taken
per region from the quiet border and the nearest grid cells.
\emph{Saddle detection} --- correlation with a 4-phase saddle template
bank ($r=5$\,px), candidates by non-maximum suppression, sub-pixel by
2D quadratic fit on the $3\times3$ response neighborhood (the same
parabola rule as the stereo matcher).
\emph{Grid assembly} --- seed at the strongest saddle, estimate a local
homography from its four lattice neighbors, grow breadth-first,
predicting each next corner through the local homography and accepting
within $0.3$ cell.
\emph{Decoding} --- sample mark presence at cell centers through the
local homography against the local black/white references; look up each
$4\times4$ window (and its rotations) in the shipped table; majority
vote fixes position and orientation.
\emph{Counter/patches} --- sample cell and patch centers the same way;
counter valid iff markers and both parity cells check; its
\code{conf} field is the minimum sampling margin
$\min_i |g_i - \tfrac12| \cdot 2 \in [0,1]$. \code{PHS} records are
emitted only for $0.15<g<0.85$ (certain straddle).
\emph{Banding} (mode M3) --- column-median intensity profile per frame;
transition rows at gradient maxima; \code{BND} pitch $=$ median
inter-transition spacing.

\paragraph{Session manifest and capture interface.} Each session
directory holds a manifest (\code{key=value} text): rig id; per camera
--- id, serial, driver, nominal fps, lens focal; display --- resolution,
pixel pitch, $T_d$; plate --- nominal $\omega$, cant angle; all RNG
seeds. The capture layer, shared verbatim with the inference sidecar
(\autoref{sec:sidecar}), is a three-call C contract:
\code{open(device)}, blocking \code{get(frame)}, \code{close()}, where a
frame is 8-bit mono pixels $+$ width/height/stride $+$ camera id $+$
driver sequence number $+$ arrival time (\code{CLOCK\_MONOTONIC} at
driver dequeue --- the timestamp whose limitations
\autoref{sec:softsync} exists to overcome). Recorded streams are PGM
sequences plus an index of \code{FRM} lines; a recorded session replays
bit-identically through reader and estimators.

\paragraph{Estimator definitions.} \emph{Orbit fit}: unknowns are the
plate axis (unit vector, 2 dof), a point on the axis (2 dof), $\omega$,
phase $\phi_0$, the fixed display-to-plate mount (6 dof), and each
camera's pose (6 dof each); the display pose at time $t$ is the mount
composed with rotation about the axis by $\omega t+\phi_0$, and the
cost is total corner reprojection error over all cameras and gated
frames, Huber-robustified at $1$\,px. Initialization: per-frame Zhang
extrinsics (\autoref{sec:zhang}) give a display-center trajectory per
camera; its plane normal initializes the axis, its circle fit
(\autoref{sec:occupancy}'s known machinery) the center, and the
\code{CTR} timeline $\omega,\phi_0$. \emph{Clock fit}: per camera,
Theil--Sen (deterministic median-of-slopes) on
(\code{CTR.count}$\cdot T_d$) vs frame index rejects counter
misdecodes, a 3-MAD gate then admits samples to least squares for
$(\phi_i, T_i)$, and \code{PHS} straddle samples jointly refine
$\phi_i$ and $t_e$ against the scanout-phase model of
\autoref{sec:tempcalib}. Acceptance bands for both are the
\autoref{sec:statrules} machinery evaluated at the rig's \emph{measured}
noise (\autoref{sec:radiometry}), not at the synthetic
$\sigma=0.3$\,px.

\paragraph{Display-app guarantees.} Fullscreen exclusive; compositor
scaling disabled and verified optically (a single-pixel checker in the
quiet border must alias to gray in any camera --- if it resolves, the
path is not 1:1); all emitted values pure 0 or 255 (gamma-immune);
counter incremented in the vsync callback, and after a missed vsync
advanced by the number of elapsed refreshes; the app logs its own
\code{DSP} record per displayed frame as the display-side ground truth.
The pattern generator itself lives in \code{libmv} (emitting frames the
app only blits), so every layer of \autoref{tab:patternspec} is
unit-testable against the reader without a display.

\paragraph{Session choreography and acceptance.} A session is:
M0 ladder $\to$ M3 ($\sim$30 frames) $\to$ M2 for $N$ revolutions
($\to$ M1 per camera only if the plate can park facing it); then the
estimator chain runs: per-camera Zhang on obliquity-gated M2 frames
$\to$ joint orbit fit $\to$ clock fit $\to$ radial alternation. The
session is \emph{accepted} only if the standing statistics land in
their bands (\autoref{sec:statrules}): reprojection T5 ratio within its
documented band; clock-fit residuals within the \autoref{ex:skew}
budget; banding-derived $\tau$ consistent across repeats; and the
resulting analytic $\F$ stored as the baseline for the run-time drift
monitor (\autoref{sec:epipolar}). Roadmap items 1--2
(\autoref{sec:roadmap}) are precisely the implementation of this
subsection.

Corresponding pixels of the two views are linked by the fundamental matrix
$\F$ through the epipolar constraint
\begin{equation}
\x_2^{\!\top}\,\F\,\x_1 = 0,
\label{eq:epiconstraint}
\end{equation}
with $\F$ of rank 2; the line $\boldsymbol{\ell}_2 = \F\x_1$ is the locus in
image 2 of possible matches of $\x_1$ (\autoref{fig:epipolar}). For
\emph{calibrated} views the essential matrix
$\E = \K_2^{\!\top}\F\,\K_1 = [\tv]_\times\R$ carries the same constraint in
normalized coordinates \cite{LonguetHiggins1981}; note the reappearance of
\eqref{eq:skew}.

Because our rig is calibrated, $\F$ is available in closed form,
\begin{equation}
\F = [\,e_2\,]_\times\, \PP_2\, \PP_1^{+},
\qquad e_2 = \PP_2\begin{pmatrix}\Cv_1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:fundamental}
\end{equation}
where $\PP_1^{+}$ is the pseudo-inverse of $\PP_1$
\cite{HartleyZisserman2004}. The system computes \eqref{eq:fundamental} once
at start-up and uses it for \emph{correspondence gating}: any candidate match
whose symmetric epipolar distance exceeds a threshold is rejected before
triangulation.

Independently, $\F$ can be \emph{estimated} from $n\ge 8$ correspondences by
the normalized 8-point algorithm \cite{Hartley1997}: each pair turns
\eqref{eq:epiconstraint} into one linear equation in the nine entries of
$\F$ --- again the homogeneous least-squares pattern of
\autoref{ex:nullvec} --- followed by rank-2 enforcement via SVD. In this
system the estimator serves two purposes: (i) \emph{health monitoring} ---
if the estimate from live correspondences drifts from the calibrated
\eqref{eq:fundamental}, the rig has been bumped or the calibration is stale;
(ii) bootstrapping geometry for \emph{uncalibrated} auxiliary photos
(\autoref{sec:extensions}). On contaminated correspondence sets the
estimator is wrapped in a RANSAC loop \cite{FischlerBolles1981} (planned
with the feature layer).

\begin{example}[essential matrix of a rectified rig]
\label{ex:essential}
Two identical cameras, the second displaced one unit along $x$:
$\R=\mat I$, $\Cv_2=(1,0,0)$, so $\tv=-\R\Cv_2=(-1,0,0)$. By
\autoref{ex:skew},
\[
\E=[\tv]_\times\R=
\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&-1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\x_2^{\!\top}\E\,\x_1
=(x_2,y_2,1)\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-y_1\end{pmatrix}
=y_2-y_1 .
\]
The epipolar constraint degenerates to $y_2=y_1$: corresponding points lie
on the \emph{same image row}. This is precisely the situation rectification
(\autoref{sec:stereo}) manufactures for arbitrary rigs.
\end{example}

\section{Triangulation}
\label{sec:triangulation}

Given observations $\x^{(v)} = (u_v, w_v)$ of the same point in $N\ge2$ views
with projection matrices $\PP^{(v)}$, each view contributes two linear
equations obtained by eliminating the projective scale from
\eqref{eq:pinhole}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}
u_v\,\PP^{(v)}_{3,:} - \PP^{(v)}_{1,:}\\[2pt]
w_v\,\PP^{(v)}_{3,:} - \PP^{(v)}_{2,:}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\X\\1\end{pmatrix} = \vect{0},
\label{eq:dlt}
\end{equation}
and stacking all views gives a $2N\times4$ homogeneous system solved, once
more, as in \autoref{ex:nullvec} --- the DLT triangulation
\cite{HartleyZisserman2004}. The implementation accepts any $N\ge2$, so
still photographs registered into the rig frame (\autoref{sec:extensions})
simply extend the same call; what additional views and additional pixels
buy, quantitatively, is analyzed in \autoref{sec:scaling}. DLT minimizes an algebraic residual; the
reprojection-optimal two-view method of Hartley--Sturm
\cite{HartleySturm1997} is a planned refinement, worthwhile mainly for
wide-baseline auxiliary views. The library reports the RMS reprojection
error of every triangulated point --- the quantity an eventual bundle
adjustment would minimize \cite{Triggs2000}.

\begin{example}[triangulation by similar triangles]
\label{ex:triangulate}
Rig of \autoref{ex:essential} with $\K$ of \autoref{ex:project}
($f=100$, $c=50$) and baseline $B=0.2$: camera 2 at $(0.2,0,0)$. Scene
point $\X=(0.1,0,2)$.
View 1: $(x,y)=(0.05,0)$, pixel $u_1=100\cdot0.05+50=55$.
View 2: camera coordinates $(0.1-0.2,\,0,\,2)=(-0.1,0,2)$, so
$u_2=100\cdot(-0.05)+50=45$.
Disparity $d=u_1-u_2=10$, and \eqref{eq:depth} gives
$Z = fB/d = 100\cdot0.2/10 = 2$~\checkmark. The lateral coordinate follows
from view 1: $X = Z\,(u_1-c_x)/f = 2\cdot5/100 = 0.1$~\checkmark --- the
full point recovered by hand.
\end{example}

\section{Rectification and dense stereo}
\label{sec:stereo}

For dense per-pixel depth the pair is first \emph{rectified}: both cameras
are rotated (synthetically, about their centers) onto a common orientation
whose $x$ axis is the baseline, making the geometry of
\autoref{ex:essential} true by construction --- epipolar lines become
identical image rows and correspondence search collapses to 1D. We use the
compact algorithm of Fusiello, Trucco and Verri \cite{Fusiello2000}: the new
rotation has rows $(\vect{x},\vect{y},\vect{z})$ with $\vect{x}$ the unit
baseline, $\vect{y}=\vect{k}\times\vect{x}$ ($\vect{k}$ the old optical axis
of camera 1) and $\vect{z}=\vect{x}\times\vect{y}$; both views share the
averaged intrinsics with zero skew, and the pixel maps are the homographies
$\mat{H}_i = \K_{\mathrm{new}}\R_{\mathrm{new}}\R_i^{\!\top}\K_i^{-1}$
(homographies again --- cf.~\eqref{eq:planehom}, here induced by pure
rotation instead of a scene plane). Since the rig is stationary,
$\mat{H}_1,\mat{H}_2$ are computed once and each video frame is warped by
table lookup.

On the rectified pair, dense correspondence is found by block matching: for
each left pixel the sum of absolute differences (SAD) over a $(2w{+}1)^2$
window is minimized along the corresponding right row over a disparity
range, followed by sub-pixel refinement by parabolic interpolation of the
cost around the minimum. This is the classic local baseline method in the
taxonomy of Scharstein and Szeliski \cite{ScharsteinSzeliski2002} --- chosen
deliberately: it is simple, portable, trivially parallel, and its failure
modes (textureless regions, occlusions, repetitive patterns) are well
understood, giving a controlled baseline against which future matchers
(rank/census transforms, semi-global aggregation) can be measured within the
same harness.

Matched disparity $d$ converts to metric depth by
\begin{equation}
Z = \frac{f\,B}{d},
\label{eq:depth}
\end{equation}
with $f$ the shared rectified focal length (pixels) and $B$ the baseline.
Differentiating \eqref{eq:depth} gives the expected depth uncertainty
\begin{equation}
\sigma_Z \;=\; \frac{Z^2}{f\,B}\,\sigma_d
\label{eq:deptherr}
\end{equation}
for disparity noise $\sigma_d$ --- the quadratic growth with $Z$ is the
fundamental accuracy law of the rig.

\begin{example}[depth error budget]
\label{ex:deptherr}
The experimental rig of \autoref{sec:results}: $f=800$\,px, $B=0.5$\,m,
mean depth $Z=4.7$\,m, per-coordinate noise $\sigma=0.3$\,px so
$\sigma_d=\sqrt2\,\sigma\approx0.42$\,px. Then
$\sigma_Z = 4.7^2\cdot 0.42/(800\cdot0.5) \approx 0.023$\,m: about
2.3\,cm of depth uncertainty --- compare the measured 2.65\,cm RMS
3D error in \autoref{tab:results}.
\end{example}

\section{From depth to models}
\label{sec:models}

Every depth estimate back-projects to a 3D point in the fixed world frame;
points are accumulated in a growable cloud with optional per-point color and
exported as ASCII PLY, directly loadable in MeshLab, CloudCompare or Blender
for inspection. This section maps out what can be built \emph{on top} of
that cloud: the representations worth targeting, how robust each is in the
literature, and the background/foreground architecture the stationary rig
makes natural.

\subsection{The representation ladder}
\label{sec:ladder}

Model types form a ladder of increasing geometric commitment; each rung is
derived from the one below, and robustness generally \emph{decreases}
upward:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Point clouds} (implemented): no topology, no commitments;
        ideal for measurement and inspection; never wrong, never finished.
  \item \emph{Occupancy / signed-distance volumes}: the truncated signed
        distance function (TSDF) fuses many noisy depth maps by running
        per-voxel averages \cite{CurlessLevoy1996}; incremental,
        noise-averaging, topology-agnostic --- the workhorse of the
        KinectFusion lineage \cite{Newcombe2011} and the natural
        accumulation structure for our per-frame depth.
  \item \emph{Triangulated meshes}: extracted from a TSDF by marching
        cubes \cite{Lorensen1987}, or from oriented points by Poisson
        reconstruction \cite{Kazhdan2006}. Both are mature and reliable
        \emph{given} rung 2; meshing raw noisy stereo clouds directly
        (Delaunay-based, ball-pivoting) is fragile and best avoided.
  \item \emph{Geometric primitives} (``something more geometric''):
        RANSAC plane extraction \cite{FischlerBolles1981} is the single
        most robust operation in the entire reconstruction literature ---
        floors, walls, table tops fall out almost for free; cylinders,
        spheres and cones follow with efficient RANSAC variants
        \cite{Schnabel2007} at the cost of tuning; box/cuboid fits give
        furniture-level abstraction. Beyond quadrics, parametric fitting
        becomes brittle.
  \item \emph{Object/semantic models} (chair, table, person): robust today
        only via learned detectors --- outside the dependency-free core,
        but the geometry below is exactly what such an external module
        would consume.
\end{enumerate}
Modern learned implicit representations (neural radiance fields, Gaussian
splatting) excel at appearance and view synthesis but are not metrically
superior for measurement, and sit outside this system's portable-C99,
controlled-environment charter.

\subsection{Background extraction: the easiest product}
\label{sec:background}

For a stationary rig, the \emph{static scene} --- walls, floor, furniture
--- is the most robustly extractable model, because time works entirely in
its favor: the per-pixel \emph{temporal median} of depth over minutes or
hours is immune to anything that moves through the scene (the same
robust-statistics trick as classical 2D background subtraction, lifted to
depth), and every additional frame averages noise down. The pipeline is:
temporal-median depth map $\to$ TSDF fusion \cite{CurlessLevoy1996}
$\to$ marching-cubes mesh \cite{Lorensen1987} of the static scene $\to$
RANSAC planes for floor/walls/table-tops $\to$ primitive fits
\cite{Schnabel2007} on the remaining clusters for furniture-level
geometry. Every stage is rung-1--4 robust. The background model is also
\emph{self-maintaining}: a piece of furniture moved to a new position is a
persistent change --- detected exactly like a lamp event in
\autoref{sec:scenarios}, but in geometry --- triggering a local update
and, as a by-product, an event log of scene rearrangements.

\subsection{Separating the movers}
\label{sec:movers}

With a background model in hand, foreground extraction is subtraction:
any depth estimate (or triangulated point) that disagrees with the
background beyond its noise band belongs to a \emph{mover}. Foreground
points are clustered by spatial proximity per frame, clusters are tracked
over time (\autoref{sec:scenarios} implements exactly this machinery),
and classification into insect / robot / human / other falls out of
crude but effective geometric statistics --- extent, height above floor,
speed, and persistence:
a single isolated point moving at $0.1$--$1$\,m/s is an insect; a
$\sim$0.3\,m-wide cluster at constant $\sim$0.1\,m height gliding on the
floor plane is the robot; a $0.4$--$0.6$\,m cluster extending
$1.5$--$2$\,m above the floor is a person. The scenario studies of
\autoref{sec:scenarios} quantify each of these tracks separately; the
unified pipeline --- background subtraction feeding one multi-target
tracker with per-class models --- is the integration goal they build
toward.

\section{Scaling the rig: more cameras and more pixels}
\label{sec:scaling}

The two-camera rig is the minimum that triangulates. This section works out
what changes when the rig grows --- $N$ cameras around the same volume, or
higher (possibly unequal) resolutions --- so that hardware decisions can be
made from the error laws instead of intuition. The library is already
shaped for this: \code{mv\_triangulate} accepts any $N\ge2$, and every
camera carries its own $\K$, so heterogeneous rigs need no new code paths in
the sparse pipeline.

\subsection{$N$ cameras: accuracy}
\label{sec:ncams}

Adding cameras improves triangulation through two distinct mechanisms.

\paragraph{Geometry (the dominant effect).} The depth law
\eqref{eq:deptherr} is governed by the baseline: rays from nearby centers
intersect at grazing angles, and the intersection point slides far along
the rays for small image perturbations. With cameras spread out, the
\emph{widest} usable pair sets the leading term: for $N$ collinear cameras
spaced $B$ apart, the widest baseline is $(N{-}1)B$ and
\begin{equation}
\sigma_Z^{\text{widest}} \;=\; \frac{Z^2}{f\,(N{-}1)B}\,\sigma_d,
\label{eq:multibaseline}
\end{equation}
an $(N{-}1)$-fold improvement before any averaging. The catch is
correspondence: wide pairs see the surface from very different angles, so
matching is harder --- which is exactly why multi-baseline stereo
\cite{OkutomiKanade1993} matches \emph{short} pairs (easy, unambiguous) and
accumulates their evidence in a common inverse-depth parameterization,
getting wide-pair accuracy at short-pair matching difficulty. Repetitive
texture that fools one pair (a periodic fence aliases at one baseline) is
disambiguated by any other baseline whose period does not divide it --- the
match must be consistent across \emph{all} pairs simultaneously.

\paragraph{Redundancy.} With visibility and geometry held fixed,
$N$ views of the same point give the usual statistical $\sqrt{N}$: the DLT
stack \eqref{eq:dlt} grows to $2N\times4$ rows and the estimate averages the
per-view noise. This is the smaller effect --- doubling $N$ buys
$\sqrt2\approx1.4\times$; doubling the \emph{spread} buys $2\times$.

\begin{example}[four collinear cameras]
\label{ex:ncams}
Extend the experimental rig of \autoref{ex:deptherr} ($f=800$\,px,
$Z=4.7$\,m, $\sigma_d=0.42$\,px) from two cameras $0.5$\,m apart to four
cameras at $0,\,0.5,\,1.0,\,1.5$\,m. The widest pair has $B=1.5$\,m, so by
\eqref{eq:multibaseline}
$\sigma_Z = 4.7^2\cdot0.42/(800\cdot1.5) \approx 7.7$\,mm --- three times
better than the 23\,mm of the original pair, from geometry alone; fusing
the remaining pairs tightens it further. The price: the widest pair's
viewing directions differ by
$2\arctan\!\bigl(0.75/4.7\bigr)\approx18^\circ$, and matching windows must
survive that foreshortening --- the Okutomi--Kanade argument for
accumulating the short pairs instead.
\end{example}

\subsection{$N$ cameras: occlusion and topology}
\label{sec:occlusion}

A point enters the model only if at least two cameras see it. With two
cameras, anything hidden from either one is lost; the reconstructed surface
is only the band that is simultaneously front-facing and unobstructed for
both --- for objects with complex topology (handles, tunnels, deep
concavities) that band can be a small fraction of the surface, and the
holes it leaves are the familiar occlusion artifacts.

A useful back-of-envelope model: if a surface point is occluded in any
single view independently with probability $q$, then
\begin{equation}
P(\text{reconstructible}) \;=\; P(\ge2\text{ of }N\text{ see it})
\;=\; 1-q^N-N\,q^{N-1}(1-q).
\label{eq:occlusionprob}
\end{equation}
Independence is optimistic for clustered cameras (they get occluded
together) and pessimistic for well-spread ones --- spreading the cameras is
what makes the assumption approachable.

\begin{example}[occlusion coverage]
\label{ex:occlusion}
Take $q=0.3$ (a fairly convoluted scene). By \eqref{eq:occlusionprob}:
$N=2$: $(1-q)^2=0.49$ --- half the surface. $N=4$:
$1-0.3^4-4\cdot0.3^3\cdot0.7 = 1-0.0081-0.0756 \approx 0.92$. $N=6$:
$1-0.3^6-6\cdot0.3^5\cdot0.7 \approx 0.99$. Going from two to four cameras
nearly doubles the reconstructible surface; four to six mops up the
remainder. The marginal camera always buys less --- but for high-genus
objects the last percent \emph{is} the topology.
\end{example}

Topology sharpens the point. Silhouette-based reasoning can never recover
concavities: the best any number of silhouettes gives is the visual hull
\cite{Laurentini1994}, which fills in bowls and blocks handles. Capturing
the true genus of an object --- the hole of a handle, the tunnel through an
arch --- requires cameras whose \emph{sightlines pass through the hole},
seeing background where the hull would put material; photo-consistency
frameworks (space carving \cite{KutulakosSeitz2000}, patch-based multiview
stereo \cite{FurukawaPonce2010}) then remove exactly the voxels/patches
contradicted by those views. So for complex topology, added cameras should
be \emph{spread to thread the object's openings and look into its
concavities}, not packed for baseline; the standard benchmark evidence
\cite{Seitz2006} is that view coverage, more than per-view quality, is what
separates complete reconstructions from holed ones. In this system's terms:
the two rig cameras provide the calibrated metric backbone, and
\autoref{sec:photos}'s registered auxiliary photos are the cheap way to add
exactly such coverage views.

Two practical costs scale with $N$: (i) calibration and synchronization ---
each camera needs its own \autoref{sec:calib} session against the same
target, all cameras a common trigger; (ii) computation --- pairwise
geometry (fundamental matrices, rectifications) grows as $N(N{-}1)/2$,
though in practice a hub-and-spoke set against one reference camera
suffices for gating.

\subsection{Resolution}
\label{sec:resolution}

For a fixed field of view, the focal length \emph{in pixels} scales with
the sensor's linear resolution: doubling from $640\times480$ to
$1280\times960$ doubles $f$ from $800$ to $1600$\,px. Two consequences
follow directly.

\paragraph{Depth precision.} Matching accuracy $\sigma_d$ is
an approximately constant \emph{fraction of a pixel} (our block matcher's
parabolic refinement sits around $0.1$--$0.3$\,px on textured surfaces), so
in \eqref{eq:deptherr} the denominator's $f$ doubles while $\sigma_d$ stays
put: $\sigma_Z$ halves. Depth precision is linear in resolution ---
until lens blur, diffraction, or per-pixel photon noise (smaller pixels
collect less light) starts inflating $\sigma_d$ and returns diminish.

\paragraph{Lateral detail.} The pixel footprint on the scene (ground
sampling distance) is $Z/f$ metres per pixel. Surface detail must span at
least $\sim$2 pixels to be matchable, so the smallest reconstructible
geometric feature is about $2Z/f$ --- this, not depth noise, is what limits
how much of a complex object's fine structure (thin ribs, small holes)
survives into the model. In the fidelity sense of
\autoref{sec:occlusion}: resolution sets the smallest feature you can
capture, view coverage sets whether you capture it at all.

\begin{example}[resolution budget]
\label{ex:resolution}
Experimental rig, $Z=4.7$\,m: footprint $Z/f = 4.7/800 = 5.9$\,mm/px,
smallest matchable feature $\approx12$\,mm, $\sigma_Z\approx23$\,mm
(\autoref{ex:deptherr}). Doubling both cameras' resolution:
footprint $2.9$\,mm/px, feature floor $\approx6$\,mm,
$\sigma_Z\approx12$\,mm. Everything improves by the same factor of two.
\end{example}

\subsection{Unequal resolutions}
\label{sec:heterogeneous}

Nothing in the model requires the cameras to match: each carries its own
$\K$ (and distortion), the fundamental matrix \eqref{eq:fundamental} exists
between any pair, and triangulation consumes pixels through each camera's
own $\PP$. What changes is the error budget. Each camera contributes
\emph{angular} noise $\sigma/f_i$ (radians); ray-intersection variance adds
them, so a pair's effective noise is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_\theta \;=\; \sqrt{(\sigma_1/f_1)^2+(\sigma_2/f_2)^2}:
\label{eq:hetero}
\end{equation}
the \emph{coarser camera dominates}, and money spent on one camera is
mostly wasted unless the other keeps up.

\begin{example}[upgrading one camera vs both]
\label{ex:hetero}
Rig of \autoref{ex:deptherr}, $\sigma=0.3$\,px per camera. Both at
$f=800$: $\sigma_\theta=\sqrt2\,(0.3/800)$. Upgrade \emph{one} camera to
$f=1600$: $\sigma_\theta=(0.3/800)\sqrt{1+\tfrac14}$, an improvement of
only $\sqrt{1.25/2}\approx0.79$ --- depth error drops from $23$\,mm to
$18$\,mm. Upgrade \emph{both}: factor $0.5$, $12$\,mm
(\autoref{ex:resolution}). Half the hardware upgrade buys about a fifth of
the benefit.
\end{example}

Two implementation notes for mixed rigs. First, rectification
(\autoref{sec:stereo}) forces a \emph{shared} $\K_{\mathrm{new}}$; our
implementation averages the two intrinsic matrices, which for very unequal
cameras oversamples the coarse image (interpolated pixels carry no new
information) --- resampling both to the coarser camera's grid keeps the
cost of dense matching honest, at the price of discarding the fine
camera's lateral detail. Second, the DLT \eqref{eq:dlt} as implemented
weights all rows equally; with strongly unequal cameras the rows of the
finer camera deserve proportionally more weight ($\propto f_i/\sigma_i$
after each view's normalization), a small planned refinement alongside the
Hartley--Sturm step (\autoref{sec:roadmap}).

\section{Planned extensions}
\label{sec:extensions}

\subsection{Auxiliary photo collections}
\label{sec:photos}

Still photographs of the same volume --- arbitrary viewpoints, possibly a
different camera --- densify coverage and fill occlusions. The integration
path follows the structure-from-motion recipe of Photo Tourism
\cite{Snavely2006}: detect scale-invariant features \cite{Lowe2004} in the
photos \emph{and} in the two calibrated video frames; match with a RANSAC
gate \cite{FischlerBolles1981}; register each photo to the rig's world frame
by resection from matches against already-triangulated points; then
re-triangulate all tracks with the registered views via the existing
$N$-view DLT (\autoref{sec:triangulation}); finally refine everything with
bundle adjustment \cite{Triggs2000}. The two calibrated cameras anchor the
metric scale --- the classic scale ambiguity of pure SfM does not arise.
Dense multiview stereo over the registered set (e.g.\ patch-based methods
\cite{FurukawaPonce2010}) is a further optional stage.

\subsection{Range-finder fusion (optional, kept open)}
\label{sec:range}

The system is designed to work without a range finder; this subsection
records what a single-beam laser ranger would add if one arrives later, so
the option stays open at zero present cost. Two integration modes:
(i) if the range finder is rigidly mounted and extrinsically calibrated to
the rig, each range sample is simply another world-frame point with small
$\sigma_Z$, fused into the cloud with inverse-variance weights; (ii) if it
produces an independent scan, the scan is registered to the vision cloud by
closed-form absolute orientation \cite{Horn1987} initialized
correspondence-free and refined with ICP \cite{BeslMcKay1992}.

\paragraph{At calibration time (with the rotating display of
\autoref{sec:rotating}).} Four distinct improvements. (i) \emph{Independent
absolute scale}: the vision-only metric chain hangs entirely on the
display's pixel pitch; a $\sim$2\,mm-accurate range at 4.7\,m is a 0.04\%
scale reference --- a second chain that turns a silent global scale bias
into a detectable discrepancy. (ii) \emph{Orbit metrology}: aimed so the
rotating screen sweeps through the beam, the periodic range trace $d(t)$
yields $\omega$, phase, orbit radius and axis distance at millimetre level,
independently of every camera, and ties laser and camera timestamps to the
same physical event. (iii) \emph{The focal--depth weak direction}
(\autoref{sec:printed}) is attacked head-on: an absolute distance to the
target plane is exactly the constraint type planar homographies lack.
(iv) \emph{The plate calibrates the laser}: if the spot is visible to the
cameras, the sweeping plate carries it through continuously varying depths;
each frame gives (range, pixel$_1$, pixel$_2$), the cameras triangulate the
spot, and the beam's ray in the rig frame ($\sim$5 DOF) falls out of a
least-squares line fit --- no manual procedure at all.

\paragraph{At run time.} Laser error is roughly constant while stereo error
grows as $Z^2$ \eqref{eq:deptherr}, so the laser anchors the far field and
the absolute scale (sparse metric skeleton, dense stereo flesh); it
provides depth in texture deserts where block matching has nothing to
match, and used as a prior it narrows the disparity search there; and a
persistent bias between laser and stereo depth at the same spot is an
online health monitor for the metric chain, complementing the $\F$-drift
monitor of \autoref{sec:epipolar}.

\begin{example}[laser--stereo crossover]
\label{ex:crossover}
With $\sigma_{\text{laser}}=2$\,mm constant and the rig's
$\sigma_Z=Z^2\sigma_d/(fB)$: equality at
$Z=\sqrt{0.002\cdot 800\cdot 0.5/0.42}\approx1.4$\,m. Nearer than that,
stereo wins per point; beyond it the laser is better along its ray ---
tenfold better at $4.7$\,m ($2$\,mm vs $23$\,mm) --- while stereo retains
the advantage of density. Inverse-variance fusion realizes both.
\end{example}

\section{Numerical implementation notes}
\label{sec:numerics}

All decompositions reduce to one kernel: a one-sided (Hestenes) Jacobi SVD
\cite{GolubVanLoan2013} for $m\ge n$ matrices, $n\le9$ in every present use
(the design matrices of \eqref{eq:planehom}, \eqref{eq:zhangconstraints},
\eqref{eq:dlt}, and the 8-point system). Jacobi SVD was chosen over
Golub--Kahan bidiagonalization for its simplicity ($\sim$100 lines, no
workspace queries), unconditional convergence, and excellent relative
accuracy on small matrices; its $O(mn^2)$-per-sweep cost is irrelevant at
these sizes. Homogeneous solutions are the right singular vector of the
smallest singular value; rank-2 enforcement for $\F$ zeroes the smallest
singular value and recomposes.

Conditioning is handled where it matters: design matrices built from raw
pixel coordinates mix terms of order $1$, $u$, and $u^2$
($u\sim10^2$ pixels), giving condition numbers around $10^8$; Hartley
normalization \cite{Hartley1997} (translate to centroid, scale to RMS
radius $\sqrt2$) reduces this to $O(10)$ and is applied in both the 8-point
and the homography estimators, with results denormalized afterwards. All
fundamental matrices are canonicalized (unit Frobenius norm,
largest-magnitude entry positive) so that analytic and estimated matrices
are directly comparable --- this is what makes the drift monitor of
\autoref{sec:epipolar} a plain matrix norm. Calibration runs Zhang's
closed form inside the small alternation loop of \autoref{sec:radial};
a full Levenberg--Marquardt refinement over all parameters is the planned
upgrade (\autoref{sec:roadmap}).

The code is strict C99 (\code{-std=c99 -pedantic -Wall -Wextra} clean),
depends only on \code{libm}, allocates only in unavoidable places (design
matrices, images, clouds), and contains no hidden state except the demos'
seeded generators. Determinism is treated as a feature: identical inputs
give bit-identical outputs across runs, so every regression is reproducible.

\section{Basic results}
\label{sec:results}

Three validation layers accompany the library. \code{tests/test\_mv.c}
checks exact properties on noiseless data (30 assertions: SVD
reconstruction and orthonormality, ray/projection consistency,
\eqref{eq:epiconstraint} to $10^{-8}$, exact homography and triangulation
recovery, exact intrinsic/pose recovery by Zhang's method, Gray-code
encode/decode round-trip, row alignment after rectification); all pass.

\subsection{Error budget and consistency: is there a bug?}

propagation is \emph{linear} in $\sigma$ in the operating regime. All
numbers below are deterministic and reproducible.

$2.82$\,px of $\sigma=0.3$: linear again.

\paragraph{Verdict.} Every check a bias-type bug could fail is passed:
all estimators are exact on noiseless data (here and in the 30-assertion
unit suite), all errors scale linearly with input noise in the operating
regime, the dominant error channel matches its analytic prediction to
$1\%$, and the calibration estimator is unbiased in expectation. The
error sources, ranked: (1) pixel noise through the depth law ---
dominant, modeled, verified; (2) estimator geometry factors (partial-arc
factor $1.2$; calibration's finite-view variance of $3.5$\,px std,
inflated further by weak pose diversity, \autoref{sec:printed});
(3) unmodeled noise \emph{anisotropy} in the circle fit --- negligible at
$0.3$\,px, dominant by $0.6$\,px; (4) DLT algebraic suboptimality and the
$3.5\sigma$ gating truncation --- both bounded below the $1\%$ level
here. A bug can never be excluded, but everything measurable currently
behaves exactly as the theory of
\autoref{sec:triangulation}--\autoref{sec:stereo} says it must.

\subsection{Statistical decision rules: when the numbers would say ``bug''}
\label{sec:statrules}

The preceding subsection can be formalized into a standing test battery.
Under the null hypothesis $H_0$ = ``implementation correct,'' the
measurement model (i.i.d.\ Gaussian pixel noise of scale $\sigma$) fixes
the sampling distribution of every summary statistic we compute; a bug is
declared when a statistic leaves its null band. Because the whole system
is deterministic, any flag is exactly reproducible --- a fired tripwire
can be replayed under a debugger.

\begin{description}
  \item[T1 --- exactness at $\sigma=0$.] Under $H_0$ every estimator is
    algebraically exact, so noiseless residuals are bounded by
    floating-point round-off amplified by conditioning,
    $\varepsilon\cdot\kappa\lesssim10^{-9}$. This test has \emph{no}
    statistical tolerance: any exceedance is a defect (sign error, wrong
    index, forgotten term). It is the single most powerful test in the
    suite and is what the 30-assertion unit suite automates.
  \item[T2 --- prediction-ratio test.] For a measured RMS over $N$
    residuals against an analytic prediction, the sample RMS under $H_0$
    fluctuates with relative standard deviation $\approx1/\sqrt{2N}$
    ($\chi^2$). Flag when $|r-1|>3/\sqrt{2N}$. A ratio that is stable but
    $\neq1$ across $\sigma$ and seeds is the signature of a wrong constant
    (missing $\sqrt2$, wrong baseline, mis-scaled $f$).
  \item[T3 --- scaling-exponent test.] Regress $\log e$ on $\log\sigma$:
    under $H_0$, slope $=1$ in the linear regime. Slope $\approx2$
    betrays a missing square root or an estimator leaving its regime;
    slope $\to0$ at small $\sigma$ betrays an additive floor --- fitting
    $e(\sigma)=\sqrt{b^2+a^2\sigma^2}$ and finding $b$ above the
    numerical floor reveals a hidden bias even when T1 cannot be run.
  \item[T4 --- ensemble bias test.] Over $M$ independent noise
    realizations, the standardized mean $|\bar e|/(s/\sqrt M)$ is
    Student-$t$ under $H_0$; flag beyond $3$. Distinguishes systematic
    bias from an unlucky draw.
  \item[T5 --- residual-consistency ($\chi^2$) test.] A least-squares fit
    with $p$ parameters against $n$ residual coordinates must leave
    RMS $=\sqrt2\,\sigma\sqrt{1-p/n}$ (per-point distance); the ratio
    fluctuates with relative std $1/\sqrt{2(n-p)}$. A ratio
    \emph{above} the band means the model or estimator is not absorbing
    what the noise model says it should (underfit, suboptimal estimator,
    contamination); \emph{below} means overfit or a too-permissive
    model.
  \item[T6 --- outlier-count test.] The epipolar gate's rejection count
    among true correspondences is Poisson with rate given by the noise
    tail beyond the gate; a large excess indicates contamination or a
    mismatched noise model, a large deficit an accidentally widened gate.
\end{description}

seeds the fitted reprojection RMS averages $0.484$\,px against the

ratio $1.17$, roughly twenty standard errors outside the null band. The
statistics are working exactly as intended: they have detected that the
alternation estimator of \autoref{sec:radial} (Zhang closed form $+$
linear radial step) is \emph{not} the joint maximum-likelihood fit ---
it minimizes algebraic surrogates, leaving $\sim17\%$ systematic residual
above the noise floor. This is the documented, expected cost of the
missing Levenberg--Marquardt stage, and it hands that roadmap item a
quantitative acceptance criterion: \emph{after LM refinement, the T5
ratio must fall to $1.00\pm0.03$}. If it does not, \emph{then} we have a
bug. This is the general pattern the project commits to: every claimed
improvement must move a named statistic into a predicted band, and every
statistic outside its band must be either explained to this standard or
treated as a defect.

\section{Scenario studies}
\label{sec:scenarios}

Four concrete monitoring scenarios, each simulated end-to-end against
ground truth in the deterministic demo style (all at the geometry tier:
points, not pixels --- detections are assumed, estimation is exercised;
each result is therefore an upper bound whose image-tier feasibility is
noted). Together they instantiate the mover-separation architecture of
\autoref{sec:movers}.

\subsection{Insect counting and entry/exit statistics}
\label{sec:insects}

Point-like targets enter the monitored box through a random face, fly an
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck random walk, and leave
(\code{demo/track\_insects.c}: 300\,s, spawn rate $0.1$/s, joint
detection probability $0.9$ per frame, \emph{plus two uniform clutter
detections per camera per frame}). Stereo association is by the epipolar
gate alone; a nearest-neighbor tracker with 3-hit confirmation and 5-miss
termination maintains identities; confirmed births/deaths map to box
faces. Results (seed 777): ground truth 31 insects, \textbf{29 tracks
confirmed} (two lived too briefly to confirm); entry/exit face
histograms match ground truth to within $\pm2$ per face; track position
RMS $24.4$\,mm --- the single-point depth law of \autoref{ex:deptherr},
as an unmodeled point target admits no $\sqrt n$ averaging. Of
$\sim$36{,}000 clutter detections, only 231 pairs slipped through the
epipolar gate and \emph{none} survived track confirmation: geometric
gating plus temporal confirmation suppresses clutter completely at these
densities. Image-tier caveat: at $4.7$\,m a 5\,mm insect subtends
$0.85$\,px --- below the $2$-px feature floor of
\autoref{sec:resolution} --- so real detection must be
temporal-differencing of sub-pixel blobs; counting accuracy will be
detection-limited, not geometry-limited.

\subsection{Diurnal cycle and artificial lighting}
\label{sec:lightscen}

The cameras double as calibrated photometers (Appendix; radiometric
calibration assumed): mean scene luminance is one number per frame.
\code{demo/lightlog.c} simulates 120 days at 1-minute resolution ---
natural light with drifting sunrise ($-1.5$\,min/day), per-day weather
($0.4$--$1.0\times$), slow AR(1) cloud noise, two lamps on noisy
schedules, two rare middle-of-the-night events --- and analyzes the sum.
Step detection (median-filter differencing): \textbf{484/484 on/off
events found, 0 missed, 33 false alarms} (cloud edges), timing RMS
$1.02$\,min, lamp identity by step magnitude \textbf{484/484 correct},
including both night events. The natural component is recovered by
subtracting the reconstructed lamp state, with a \emph{nightly
re-anchor} (pre-dawn darkness forces the natural component to zero ---
a physical constraint that stops false-alarm error from propagating
across days). Sunrise is then estimated per day by two-level template
inversion (crossings of 15\% and 35\% of the day's robust peak invert
the known diurnal shape analytically; amplitude and shape cancel):
offset $0.7$\,min (unbiased), day-to-day jitter $25.3$\,min RMS ---
the genuine information floor imposed by 100-min-correlated cloud
cover, not an estimator defect --- and the seasonal drift is recovered
as \[ \widehat{\text{drift}} = -1.61\ \text{min/day} \quad
(\text{true } -1.50,\ \sigma_{\text{slope}} \approx 0.07). \]
A 30-day run is statistically \emph{underpowered} for this drift
($\sigma_{\text{slope}}\approx0.7$): a season of data is what makes the
trend measurable --- the T4 logic of \autoref{sec:statrules} applied to
experiment design.

\subsection{Human occupancy}
\label{sec:occupancy}

A known three-person cohort (heights $1.62/1.75/1.88$\,m) makes 13
scheduled visits over a simulated day (\code{demo/track\_people.c}):
enter at the door, walk to desk or window, linger, exit. Each person is
a cylinder with self-occluding torso-band features plus a head point;
per frame the known-radius circle fit gives ground position and the
triangulated head gives height. Results (seed 31337): \textbf{13/13
episodes detected, 13/13 identities correct} (nearest cohort height;
the $130$\,mm height gaps dwarf the $0.3$\,mm height RMS), 13/13
trajectory targets correct, ground-position RMS $14.1$\,mm, entry
latency $0.2$\,s (the 3-frame confirmation), per-person occupancy
minutes recovered to $0.1$\,min. Caveats: identity-by-height is valid
only for small cohorts with distinct heights (it is a household
biometric, not a general one), and the idealized head/torso detections
make these upper bounds --- real image-tier person detection is where
the learned-detector boundary of \autoref{sec:ladder} sits.

\subsection{What the scenarios share}

All four reuse the same small kernel --- epipolar gating, triangulation,
model-constrained fitting, temporal confirmation --- plus one scenario
layer each (multi-target association; photometric step decomposition;
identity classification). The common upgrades they all point to:
constant-velocity Kalman filtering (speed estimates,
\autoref{tab:trackresults}), the Mahalanobis-weighted fits of
\autoref{sec:budget}, and the background-subtraction front end of
\autoref{sec:movers}.

\section{Person detection and face recognition with open-source components}
\label{sec:faces}

The learned-detector boundary drawn in \autoref{sec:ladder} does not have
to remain outside the system: mature open-source detectors and face
recognizers can be attached \emph{without} touching the dependency-free
core, and the occupancy scenario (\autoref{sec:occupancy}) already defines
the exact seam they plug into. This section records the component
landscape, the integration architecture, and --- the question that
determines what to promise --- how accuracy differs between a
pre-registered cohort and unknown faces.

\subsection{Component landscape}
\label{sec:osscomponents}

Three roles, each with solid open-source fillings:
\emph{person detection} --- YOLO-family single-shot detectors
\cite{Redmon2018} (the original darknet implementation is plain C;
Apache-licensed successors such as YOLOX run everywhere), or classical
HOG pedestrian detection in OpenCV as a weak-but-tiny fallback;
\emph{face detection + landmarks} --- RetinaFace \cite{Deng2020} or its
lightweight successors (SCRFD, YuNet), which return a box plus five
landmarks (eyes, nose, mouth corners); \emph{face embedding} ---
deep metric networks mapping an aligned $112\times112$ crop to a unit
vector such that same-person pairs are close: FaceNet established the
recipe \cite{Schroff2015}, ArcFace-class models are the current
open-source standard \cite{Deng2019}. The practical integration seam for
all three is an ONNX-exported model behind the ONNX Runtime C API
(MIT-licensed, single shared library, plain-C interface) --- the one
boundary object between the C99 world and the learned world. License
note: several popular YOLO packagings are AGPL; the Apache/MIT
alternatives keep the system's licensing simple.

\subsection{Piping it in}
\label{sec:sidecar}

The architecture is a \emph{sidecar oracle}: the core stays pure C99 and
treats the detector exactly as the scenario demos treat their simulated
detections. Per frame and per camera, the sidecar consumes the image and
emits rows of \code{(frame, camera, class, box, landmarks, embedding)} ---
a text/CSV stream over a pipe or file, trivially parseable, recordable,
and replayable (determinism is preserved by recording the sidecar's
output once and replaying it in tests --- the oracle's answers become
fixtures). The geometric core then does what it already does:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{stereo association}: detections in the two cameras are
        paired by the epipolar gate on box centers/landmarks
        (\autoref{sec:epipolar}) --- the same gate that suppressed
        clutter in \autoref{sec:insects};
  \item \emph{triangulation}: paired boxes give the person's 3D position;
        paired eye landmarks give a metric face position and, with the
        floor plane, the height biometric of \autoref{sec:occupancy};
  \item \emph{track-level identity}: embeddings are not judged per frame
        but accumulated per \emph{track} (quality-weighted average of
        embeddings across the track's frames): the tracker turns many
        marginal glimpses into one confident identity, and the identity
        then travels with the track through frames where the face is
        turned away entirely.
\end{enumerate}
Identity fusion is a small score-level combination: face-embedding
similarity where a face is visible, the height/gait geometric cues
otherwise --- the heterogeneous-evidence pattern of the appendix
(\autoref{sec:blend}) applied to identity.

\subsection{Pre-registered vs unknown faces}
\label{sec:openset}

The accuracy question splits into three regimes with \emph{very}
different numbers.

\paragraph{Verification (1:1) and closed-set identification (1:$N$,
person guaranteed enrolled).} On the classic verification benchmark LFW
\cite{Huang2007} modern open-source models exceed $99.5\%$ --- the
benchmark is saturated and mostly measures that nothing is broken. The
more honest unconstrained figure is IJB-C \cite{Maze2018} verification at
strict false-accept rates: ArcFace-class models reach a true-accept rate
around $96$--$97\%$ at FAR $=10^{-4}$ \cite{Deng2019}. For a
\emph{pre-registered household cohort} --- $N$ of order 10, controlled
enrollment photos, closed-set --- rank-1 identification is effectively
$\ge99\%$ whenever the probe image is adequate: with guaranteed gallery
membership, the decision is an argmax among a few well-separated
identities, and errors are driven almost entirely by probe quality, not
confusion.

\paragraph{Open-set identification (the person may be unknown).} This is
a different problem: the system must first decide \emph{whether} the
probe is anyone enrolled, which requires thresholding an absolute
similarity score rather than taking an argmax. Impostor scores from
unknown faces overlap the tail of genuine scores, so every operating
point trades false alarms (visitor labeled as a resident) against misses
(resident labeled unknown). NIST's identification evaluations
\cite{Grother2019} quantify the cost: even leading algorithms give up
substantial accuracy relative to closed-set --- at a false-positive
identification rate of $1\%$, miss rates range from a few percent with
high-quality frontal probes to \emph{tens of percent} with
surveillance-quality probes, and degradation grows with gallery size and
probe--gallery quality gap. The rule of thumb this system should adopt:
\textbf{closed-set household identification is a solved problem at our
scale; reliable ``this is a stranger'' detection is not free} --- it
needs a margin-calibrated threshold, benefits enormously from track-level
embedding fusion, and should be reported with its false-alarm/miss
operating point, never as a single accuracy number.

\paragraph{The resolution gate.} All of the above presumes enough face
pixels, and here our own \autoref{sec:resolution} bites hard.

\begin{example}[face pixels at range]
\label{ex:facepixels}
A face is $\approx0.20$\,m tall with $0.064$\,m interocular distance. At
the experimental rig's $f=800$\,px and $Z=4.7$\,m: face height
$0.20\cdot800/4.7\approx34$\,px, interocular
$\approx11$\,px --- far below the $\gtrsim80$--$112$\,px face crops the
embedding networks are built for; expect severely degraded embeddings.
For a $100$\,px face at $4.7$\,m the rig needs
$f=100\cdot4.7/0.20=2350$\,px --- a 4K-class sensor, or a normal sensor
with a narrow-field lens.
\end{example}

The systems answer follows the appendix's blending argument
(\autoref{sec:blend}): do not demand identity everywhere. Add one
\emph{identity camera} --- a narrow-field unit aimed at the natural
chokepoint (the door of \autoref{sec:occupancy}, where every track
begins and ends) --- capture the face at high resolution once per entry,
and let the tracker carry that identity through the wide-angle geometric
volume. Identity becomes an attribute assigned at a favorable moment and
\emph{propagated by geometry}, rather than a per-frame recognition
burden. A final scope note befitting the controlled environment: the
enrollment gallery is the household's own, processing is local, and the
open-set threshold should be set to prefer ``unknown'' over
misattribution.

\section{Camera hardware and the quality of data}
\label{sec:hardware}

The capture hardware determines which error terms enter the pipeline before
any algorithm runs. The organizing observation: cheap capture hardware
mostly adds \emph{biases} --- rolling shear, synchronization parallax,
compression structure, unmodelable distortion, focus creep --- rather than
noise. Noise we model and budget (\autoref{ex:deptherr}); biases survive
averaging, masquerade as geometry, and poison calibration silently. The
specifications below are therefore ranked by which biases they admit.

\subsection{Shutter: global vs rolling}
\label{sec:shutter}

A global-shutter sensor exposes all pixels over one common interval --- each
frame is a true snapshot, described by a single $\PP$. A rolling-shutter
sensor exposes and reads row by row: the bottom row of a frame is captured
10--30\,ms after the top row. For a static scene the difference vanishes;
for anything moving, each row samples a different instant and no single
projection matrix describes the frame --- the camera model of
\autoref{sec:camera} itself breaks.

\begin{example}[rolling-shutter shear on the rotating target]
\label{ex:rolling}
The rotating display of \autoref{sec:rotating} moves at
$\approx27$\,px/s in the image. A rolling readout of $20$\,ms samples the
top and bottom of the target $20$\,ms apart: a shear of
$27\cdot0.02\approx0.5$\,px across the corner grid --- larger than the
$0.3$\,px noise budget, and \emph{systematic}: it does not average away
over frames but feeds a consistently wrong homography into calibration.
\end{example}

\subsection{Synchronization: hardware trigger vs free-running}
\label{sec:trigger}

A trigger line starts exposure on all cameras from one electrical pulse
(coincidence to microseconds). Free-running cameras can only be matched by
software timestamps --- a few \emph{milliseconds} of jitter and drift.
Triangulation assumes both cameras saw the point at the same instant; a
time offset $\Delta t$ on a point moving at speed $v$ creates a phantom
disparity $f\,v\,\Delta t/Z$ that is mathematically indistinguishable from
depth.

\begin{example}[the price of software synchronization]
\label{ex:sync}
An object crossing the experimental rig at $1$\,m/s with $5$\,ms software
sync: phantom disparity $=800\cdot(1\cdot0.005)/4.7\approx0.85$\,px, which
the depth law \eqref{eq:deptherr} converts to
$\approx47$\,mm of depth error --- exceeding the rig's entire
$23$\,mm error budget at that range. For dynamic scenes, synchronization
error dominates every other term; a trigger wire outperforms any camera
upgrade. The trigger also permits phase-locking exposure against mains
flicker and locking capture to the display's frame counter
(\autoref{sec:rotating}).
\end{example}

\subsection{Software synchronization: measuring and correcting the clocks}
\label{sec:softsync}

This subsection is the practice counterpart of the temporal camera model
\eqref{eq:clock} of \autoref{sec:timemodel}: how the clock parameters
$(\phi_i, T_i)$ are actually measured and their effect corrected. When no
trigger line exists (the consumer tier), frames from independent,
possibly heterogeneous cameras are never simultaneous: free-running
30\,fps streams differ by up to half a frame period ($|\Delta t|$
averaging $\sim$8\,ms), and consumer crystals drift 50--100\,ppm, so the
offset slides several milliseconds per minute --- it must be
\emph{tracked}, not calibrated once. Since \autoref{ex:sync} showed the
cost lands entirely on movers (static structure is indifferent to
$\Delta t$), the right treatment is not to chase alignment but to make
time part of the measurement model. Three layers:

\paragraph{Bound.} Pair frames nearest-neighbor in time rather than by
index: free, and caps $|\Delta t|$ at half a period under any drift.

\paragraph{Measure.} Model each camera's clock as offset $+$ skew
against a common reference and estimate both continuously, two
independent ways. (i) \emph{The dynamic display pattern}
(\autoref{sec:rotating}): the Gray-coded counter gives frame-level
display timestamps (record \code{CTR} of \autoref{tab:records}); the
phase patches refine them below the refresh period (\code{PHS}); a
linear fit of (camera frame index, decoded display time) pairs per
camera yields its offset and rate --- see the precision budget in
\autoref{ex:skew}. (ii) \emph{Movers themselves}: a tracked point moving
across the epipolar field sits off its epipolar line by (image velocity
$\perp$ line)$\,\times\Delta t$; with $\F$ known exactly, $\Delta t$ is a
one-parameter fit to residuals already computed --- the $\F$-drift
monitor of \autoref{sec:epipolar} with one extra parameter, usable at
run time when no display is present. (A $\mu$C-driven blinking LED in a
frame corner is the minimal permanent clock beacon if continuous
display-free measurement is wanted.) A deliberate small rate offset on
one camera ($\sim$0.1\,Hz) makes the phases \emph{beat}, sweeping
$\Delta t$ through zero every $\sim$10\,s: the ``golden pairs'' at each
null (aligned to $\sim$50\,$\mu$s) are free validation samples for the
estimator.

\paragraph{Correct.} Give every measurement its own timestamp and
interpolate \emph{tracked detections} (not images) onto the common
clock before triangulating; the residual is second-order
(\autoref{ex:interp}). Dense stereo needs no correction at all --- the
dense content is the static background; movers travel the tracked
path, which is exactly the path that interpolates. The principled limit
for fast movers is trajectory triangulation: fit a short motion model to
$K$ timestamped rays rather than intersecting two rays at a fictitious
common instant --- and the same per-measurement-timestamp machinery is
what rolling-shutter correction (\autoref{sec:shutter}) consumes, so
building it once serves both. Self-verification in the
\autoref{sec:statrules} style: the slope of epipolar residual vs image
speed must equal the fitted $\Delta t$, and golden pairs must show none.

\begin{example}[clock-fit precision from the display]
\label{ex:skew}
Display timestamps are quantized to the refresh period
($16.7$\,ms at 60\,Hz), i.e.\ per-sample noise
$\sigma_q=16.7/\sqrt{12}\approx4.8$\,ms. One minute at 30\,fps gives
$N=1800$ samples: offset precision $\sigma_q/\sqrt N\approx0.11$\,ms,
and rate precision
$\sigma_q\sqrt{12/N}/T\approx7$\,ppm over the $T=60$\,s span ---
comfortably resolving 50--100\,ppm crystals in one minute, before the
phase patches sharpen $\sigma_q$ further.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[interpolation makes sync second-order]
\label{ex:interp}
A walker at $1$\,m/s with a full half-frame mismatch
($\Delta t=16.7$\,ms) uncorrected: phantom depth error
$\approx150$\,mm by \autoref{ex:sync}. Linearly interpolating the
tracked detection between frames leaves only the curvature term
$a\,\Delta t^2/8$: at $a=2$\,m/s$^2$,
$2\cdot0.0167^2/8\approx70\,\mu$m --- a factor of $\sim$2000. If
$\Delta t$ itself is known to $\pm1$\,ms (\autoref{ex:skew}), the
residual first-order term is $\sim$1\,mm at $1$\,m/s: software
synchronization turns the dominant dynamic-scene error into a minor one.
\end{example}

The verdict, stated as a rule: on the consumer tier, synchronization
mismatch is quantified and corrected, never assumed away --- one more
instance of this appendix's theme that a \emph{modeled} defect becomes a
term of the estimator rather than a bias. The trigger tier
(\autoref{sec:trigger}) remains the clean answer where the hardware
allows it.

\subsection{Radiometric and noise measurement procedures}
\label{sec:radiometry}

The cameras are the rig's own best measuring instruments; these four
procedures, run once per rig (modes M0/flat scenes,
\autoref{tab:modes}), produce the numbers everything else consumes.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Photon-transfer curve} $\to$ the noise scale $\sigma$.
        Capture repeated frames of a static scene at several exposure
        levels; per-pixel temporal variance vs mean is linear with slope
        $1/\text{gain}$ (e$^-$/DN) and intercept the read-noise floor.
        The resulting intensity noise, propagated through the matcher,
        is the rig's \emph{measured} $\sigma$ --- the number that
        re-scales every acceptance band of \autoref{sec:statrules}
        (which this document evaluates at the synthetic $0.3$\,px).
  \item \emph{Flicker audit.} High-rate (or exposure-phase-swept)
        capture of a blank wall; the FFT of mean intensity at
        $2f_{\text{mains}}$ (100/120\,Hz) gives the modulation depth.
        Rule: keep it below $\sim$1--2\%, or set
        $t_e = n/(2f_{\text{mains}})$, which nulls it exactly.
  \item \emph{Inter-camera radiometric transfer.} A gray card (or the
        display's M0 ladder) viewed by both cameras; the joint
        histogram gives the gain/offset map between them. Flat and
        linear clears plain SAD matching; visible curvature is the
        signal to switch the matcher to a rank/census cost.
  \item \emph{Flat-field / vignetting.} The display showing uniform
        gray (M0), imaged defocused; the per-pixel gain map is stored
        and divides all subsequent frames; it also supplies the local
        white references the reader's decoding rules assume
        (\autoref{sec:calibspec}).
\end{enumerate}

A cheap lux meter anchors the ladder in absolute units so results
transfer between sites; the display's off-axis brightness falloff ---
measured for free as the plate sweeps (\autoref{sec:rotating}) ---
completes the radiometric picture of the fixture itself.

\subsection{Interface: UVC vs machine-vision stacks}
\label{sec:uvc}

UVC (USB Video Class) is the ``any webcam just works'' protocol
(\code{v4l2} on Linux, no vendor SDK). Its convenience reflects its
purpose --- streaming video to humans: at higher resolutions frames
usually arrive \emph{MJPEG-compressed}, and JPEG's $8\times8$ block
artifacts are structured noise at exactly the spatial frequencies where
sub-pixel corners and block matching live --- a \emph{correlated}
contribution to $\sigma_d$ that does not average like photon noise. UVC
also offers only coarse loosely-specified exposure/gain controls,
imprecise timestamps, and no trigger support in the class. Machine-vision
interfaces (USB3 Vision/GenICam via vendor SDKs) deliver raw pixels,
physical-unit controls, per-frame hardware timestamps and trigger I/O.
Rule of thumb: UVC is acceptable exactly where rolling shutter is
acceptable --- static bring-up.

\subsection{Optics: M12 vs C/CS mounts}
\label{sec:lenses}

M12 (``S-mount'') lenses are the small threaded board lenses of
webcam-class hardware: cheap, fixed aperture, focused by rotating the
barrel, no standardized flange distance. C-mount and CS-mount are the
machine-vision standards: metal barrels, multi-element glass, iris and
focus rings with lock screws, standardized flange distances (C:
$17.53$\,mm; CS: $12.53$\,mm; a C lens fits a CS body via a 5\,mm spacer,
not conversely). Three separate data-quality channels:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Sharpness (MTF) is the true floor under $\sigma_d$}:
        sub-pixel localization precision scales with the optical blur
        radius; a soft lens can double $\sigma_d$, and via
        \eqref{eq:deptherr} double $\sigma_Z$ --- equivalent to discarding
        half the sensor resolution (cf.\ the weakest-link law of
        \autoref{sec:heterogeneous}: the lens is a full member of that
        chain).
  \item \emph{Distortion modelability}: the Brown model
        \eqref{eq:distortion} absorbs the smooth distortion of decent
        lenses; cheap wide M12s exhibit higher-order or non-radial terms
        that leave \emph{systematic} calibration residuals.
  \item \emph{Stability of $\K$}: thread-focused plastic barrels creep
        with vibration and temperature --- silent recalibration events,
        precisely what the $\F$-drift monitor of \autoref{sec:epipolar}
        exists to catch. Locked C/CS rings hold $\K$ for months.
        Autofocus is this failure mode as a feature: \emph{a calibrated
        camera must never refocus}.
\end{enumerate}

An iris adds one more useful knob: most lenses peak in sharpness around
f/4--f/8; beyond that diffraction itself blurs (spot
$\approx1.22\,\lambda N$: at f/8 about $5.4\,\mu$m, already exceeding a
$3\,\mu$m pixel).

\subsection{Monochrome vs color}
\label{sec:mono}

A Bayer color sensor samples each color at $\tfrac14$--$\tfrac12$ of the
pixel grid and interpolates the rest; demosaicing smears exactly the
high-contrast edges that corners and matching depend on, and costs about
one stop of sensitivity. This pipeline processes grayscale throughout, so
monochrome sensors deliver strictly better data; color remains at most a
redundancy layer in the calibration pattern (\autoref{sec:rotating}).

\subsection{Blending heterogeneous cameras}
\label{sec:blend}

Knowing each camera's characteristics is itself an asset: a
\emph{modeled} defect is a correctable or at least gateable one. A
rolling-shutter camera whose row-readout time is known can have its rows
time-stamped individually and its observations gated out (or motion-
compensated) whenever the scene moves; a camera with a measured noise
curve enters the triangulation with an honest weight
(\autoref{sec:heterogeneous}); a lens with well-fit distortion
contributes unbiased rays while one with known residuals can be trusted
only in its image center. The measurement model becomes per-camera, and
every characteristic in \autoref{tab:hardware} turns from a hidden bias
into a term of that model.

A deliberate \emph{blend} of camera types then does more than average ---
it makes artifacts detectable, because most artifacts are
\emph{camera-specific} while the scene is common. MJPEG block edges,
rolling shear, demosaic fringes, and specular highlights each live in
some cameras and not others (highlights move with viewpoint; compression
artifacts sit on one camera's grid); true structure must be consistent
across all views. With identical cameras, a shared systematic error is
invisible --- every unit votes for the same lie; with heterogeneous
hardware the correlation is broken and disagreement \emph{localizes} the
artifact, in the same spirit as the $\F$-drift and laser-audit health
monitors (\autoref{sec:epipolar}, \autoref{sec:range}). Complementary
pairings fall out naturally: a synchronized global-shutter mono pair as
the metric backbone; a high-resolution color camera (rolling shutter
tolerated, gated by motion) contributing fine texture and appearance to
the model; a wide-FOV unit for coverage and moving-object flagging, its
geometry weighted down per \autoref{sec:heterogeneous}. Geometry from
the cameras built for geometry, appearance from the cameras built for
appearance, and cross-checks from their disagreement --- the
$N$-camera analysis of \autoref{sec:scaling} applies unchanged, since
every camera carries its own $\K$, distortion, and noise model.

\section{Root-mean-square (RMS) error: definition and use}
\label{sec:rms}

Nearly every quantitative claim in this document is an RMS figure. This
appendix defines it, explains why it is the right summary here, and maps
every way the document uses it.

\subsection{Definition}
For scalar errors $e_1,\dots,e_N$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMS} \;=\; \sqrt{\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^N e_i^2},
\label{eq:rmsdef}
\end{equation}
the square root of the mean of the squares. For vector errors (a 2D
reprojection offset, a 3D position error) the same formula is applied to
the \emph{norms}: $\mathrm{RMS}=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum\|\vect e_i\|^2}$,
which is also $\sqrt{\sum_k \mathrm{RMS}_k^2}$ over the components ---
components add \emph{in quadrature}.

\begin{example}[RMS vs mean vs max]
\label{ex:rmsbasic}
Errors $\{1,2,2,5\}$: mean $=2.5$; RMS
$=\sqrt{(1+4+4+25)/4}=\sqrt{8.5}\approx2.92$; max $=5$. RMS always lies
between mean and max, weighting large errors more than the mean does
(squaring makes the $5$ contribute $25/34$ of the total). This document
reports RMS as the headline and max separately when tails matter
(e.g.\ \autoref{tab:trackresults}).
\end{example}

\subsection{Why squares}
Three reasons, in increasing depth. (i) \emph{Calculus}: squared error is
smooth, so least-squares problems have closed forms --- every estimator in
this system (\autoref{ex:nullvec} onward) minimizes a sum of squares, and
RMS is exactly the quantity those estimators optimize, expressed per
sample. (ii) \emph{Probability}: for zero-mean Gaussian noise the RMS of
the errors converges to the distribution's $\sigma$; reporting RMS is
reporting the fitted noise scale, directly comparable to the injected
$\sigma$ in every experiment. Least squares \emph{is} maximum likelihood
under Gaussian noise, closing the loop. (iii) \emph{Composition}:
independent error sources add in quadrature,
$\sigma_{\text{tot}}^2=\sum_j\sigma_j^2$ --- the working rule behind the
two-coordinate pixel distance, the two-camera angular noise
\eqref{eq:hetero}, and the disparity noise $\sigma_d=\sqrt2\,\sigma$ used
throughout.

\begin{example}[quadrature]
\label{ex:rmsquad}
Per-coordinate pixel noise $\sigma=0.3$\,px: the Euclidean reprojection
\emph{distance} has RMS $\sqrt{0.3^2+0.3^2}=0.424$\,px --- the baseline
against which the residuals of \autoref{sec:statrules} are judged.
Disparity, a difference of two independent $0.3$\,px coordinates,
likewise has $\sigma_d=\sqrt2\cdot0.3\approx0.42$\,px
(\autoref{ex:deptherr}).
\end{example}

\subsection{Bias, variance, and what RMS cannot tell you}
RMS mixes systematic and random error:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMS}^2 \;=\; \underbrace{\bar e^{\,2}}_{\text{bias}^2}
\;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac1N\sum (e_i-\bar e)^2}_{\text{variance}} .
\label{eq:biasvar}
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[decomposition]
\label{ex:rmsbias}
A constant bias of $3$ plus zero-mean noise of standard deviation $4$
gives $\mathrm{RMS}=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$. Two very different failure modes,
one number. This is precisely why the diagnostics of \autoref{sec:budget}
never rely on a single RMS: the $\sigma$-sweep separates the terms ---
variance scales with $\sigma$, bias does not --- and the $\sigma=0$ run
isolates bias exactly.
\end{example}

\subsection{Statistical handling}
Two facts about RMS as an \emph{estimate}, both load-bearing in
\autoref{sec:statrules}. First, a sample RMS over $N$ Gaussian errors
fluctuates with relative standard deviation $\approx1/\sqrt{2N}$
($\chi^2$ with $N$ degrees of freedom): with $N=250$ that is $4.5\%$,
which sets the $\pm3/\sqrt{2N}$ null bands of the T2/T5 tripwires.
Second, residuals of a least-squares fit are \emph{smaller} than the
noise, because the fit absorbs $p$ degrees of freedom:
$\mathrm{RMS}_{\text{resid}}=\sigma\sqrt{1-p/n}$ for $p$ parameters and
$n$ residual coordinates. Triangulation ($p=3$, $n=4$) leaves
$\sigma\sqrt{1/4}\cdot\sqrt2=\sigma/\sqrt2$ per-view distance ---
$0.212$\,px at $\sigma=0.3$, matching the measured $0.218$
(\autoref{tab:results}); calibration ($p=42$, $n=756$) should leave
$0.412$\,px, and the measured $0.484$ is the fired T5 flag of
\autoref{sec:statrules}. An RMS \emph{below} the corrected prediction
signals overfitting just as surely as one above signals underfitting.

\subsection{Where this document uses RMS}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Reprojection RMS} (px): residual image distance after
        triangulation or calibration --- the fitted noise scale;
        compare to $\sqrt2\,\sigma\sqrt{1-p/n}$
        (\autoref{tab:results}, \autoref{tab:calibresults}).
  \item \emph{3D / position RMS} (m or mm): Euclidean reconstruction or
        tracking error against ground truth; components reported
        separately when anisotropy matters (depth vs lateral,
        \autoref{tab:diagtrack}).
  \item \emph{Timing RMS} (s or min): event and sunrise timing in the
        scenario studies (\autoref{sec:scenarios}); same formula, time
        errors instead of distances.
  \item \emph{Jitter RMS about an offset}: variance-only RMS after
        removing a known constant (the sunrise estimator's offset,
        \autoref{sec:lightscen}) --- i.e.\ the second term of
        \eqref{eq:biasvar} alone, used deliberately when the bias is
        understood and harmless.
  \item \emph{Predicted RMS}: the error laws \eqref{eq:deptherr},
        \eqref{eq:hetero} are statements about the RMS of infinite
        repetitions; measured-over-predicted ratios are the consistency
        statistics of \autoref{sec:statrules}.
\end{itemize}
